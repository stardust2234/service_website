Running a small business often means wearing more hats than you expected.

You start out doing the work you actually set up the business to do. Then, somewhere along the way, you're answering emails, booking appointments, sending invoices, organising receipts, updating spreadsheets and chasing people for information.

None of those jobs seems particularly big on its own.

But together? They can quietly take over your week.

If you're spending evenings catching up on paperwork or constantly switching between your actual work and “just one quick admin task”, it might be time to decide what really needs to be done by you.

Here are 12 tasks you don't necessarily need to handle yourself.

## 1. Managing your inbox
A busy inbox can become a job in itself.

Sorting messages, identifying what needs your attention, filing correspondence and following up on unanswered emails all take time.

Someone else can help keep things organised, flag the messages that genuinely need you and deal with routine enquiries.

You still stay in control without needing to live in your inbox.

## 2. Managing your calendar
Booking appointments sounds simple until you're juggling customers, meetings, travel time, cancellations and rescheduling.

Calendar management can include:

- arranging appointments
- confirming bookings
- rescheduling meetings
- blocking out important work time
- sending reminders
- keeping different calendars organised

Instead of going back and forth finding a suitable time, you can concentrate on the appointment itself.

## 3. Following up with customers
Not every customer follows up when they say they will.

Quotes get forgotten. Documents don't arrive. Messages go unanswered.

Keeping track of those loose ends is important, but you don't necessarily need to be the person chasing every one of them.

A simple follow-up process can make sure things keep moving without relying on you remembering everything.

## 4. Preparing and sending invoices
You've finished the work. Now you need to get paid.

But first you have to create the invoice, check the details, send it, record it and keep track of whether it's actually been paid.

When you're busy, invoicing is one of those jobs that's surprisingly easy to put off.

Having a regular invoicing process helps make sure completed work turns into invoices promptly rather than becoming another Friday-evening job.

## 5. Keeping track of unpaid invoices
Sending an invoice is only half the job.

Someone also needs to know what's been paid and what hasn't.

Keeping an organised record of outstanding invoices makes it easier to spot overdue payments and send appropriate reminders.

It also means you don't have to search through your bank account and emails every time you're wondering whether a customer has paid.

## 6. Organising receipts and expenses
Receipts have an impressive ability to end up everywhere.

Your inbox. Your wallet. A carrier bag. A downloads folder called “receipts”. Possibly the glovebox.

A simple system for collecting and recording expenses throughout the month can save a much bigger sorting job later.

This could include organising digital receipts, recording expenses and making sure supporting documents are stored somewhere sensible.

Your future self and your accountant will probably appreciate it.

## 7. Keeping your bookkeeping up to date
Bookkeeping is much easier when it's kept up to date rather than reconstructed months later.

Routine bookkeeping can include recording transactions, categorising income and expenses, reconciling accounts and keeping financial records organised.

You should still understand what's happening with your business finances.

You just don't necessarily need to enter every transaction yourself.

## 8. Updating customer records
Customer information changes.

New enquiries arrive. Contact details need updating. Jobs progress. Notes need recording.

Whether you use a CRM, spreadsheet or another system, it only remains useful if somebody keeps it current.

Routine data entry and record maintenance are good examples of necessary work that doesn't always require the business owner's time.

## 9. Preparing quotes and standard documents
If you regularly create similar quotes, proposals, letters or forms, you may be repeating the same process unnecessarily.

Templates can make much of that work quicker.

Someone can prepare the standard parts, check that the right information has been included and leave you to review anything that genuinely requires your expertise.

## 10. Organising files and documents
Finding a document shouldn't require detective work.

A sensible folder structure and consistent file naming can make contracts, invoices, customer documents and business records much easier to find.

It's not glamorous work.

But neither is spending 20 minutes searching for FINAL_quote_new_v2_revised.pdf.

## 11. Booking and coordinating appointments
Depending on your business, you might need to coordinate customers, suppliers, contractors or members of your team.

That can mean confirming availability, sending information, rearranging appointments and making sure everyone knows where they're supposed to be.

The coordination can often be handled for you while you remain involved where your decision is actually needed.

## 12. Keeping recurring admin on schedule
Some business tasks aren't difficult.

They just need to happen.

Every week. Every month. Every quarter.

That might include updating records, checking outstanding invoices, preparing information for your accountant, reviewing appointments or following up with customers.

Turning these into recurring processes means fewer things have to live in your head.

## So, what should you delegate first?

You don't need to hand over everything at once.

Start by looking for tasks that are **repetitive, time-consuming and don't require your particular expertise.**

For the next week, make a note whenever you find yourself saying:

*“I'll quickly do this first.”*

Those five-minute jobs add up.

If you're spending several hours every week managing emails, appointments, invoices, records and paperwork, ask yourself:

**Does this task actually need me, or does it simply need to get done?**

That's a useful distinction.

There will always be parts of your business that need your judgement, relationships and expertise. The goal of delegating isn't to lose control of them.

It's to create more room for them.
