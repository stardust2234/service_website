Bookkeeper. Accountant.

They both deal with numbers, they can both help with your business finances, and sometimes their work overlaps.

So what's the difference and do you actually need both?

The simplest way to think about it is this:

**A bookkeeper helps keep your financial records organised and up to date. An accountant uses those records to help with things such as accounts, tax and financial advice.**

For a small business, knowing where one role ends and the other begins can help you work out what kind of support you actually need.

## What does a bookkeeper do?
Bookkeeping is about keeping an accurate record of the money moving through your business.

Depending on the business and the level of support required, that might include:
- recording income and expenses
- categorising transactions
- reconciling bank accounts
- keeping receipts and financial documents organised
- recording invoices and payments
- keeping track of outstanding invoices
- maintaining bookkeeping records
- preparing organised information for your accountant

The aim is to make sure your records reflect what's actually happening in the business.

And importantly, to avoid reaching the end of the year with twelve months of transactions to untangle.

## What does an accountant do?
An accountant will generally work at a different level.

Rather than concentrating primarily on maintaining your day-to-day records, an accountant may use those records to prepare accounts, calculate tax liabilities and provide financial or tax advice.

Depending on their services and qualifications, an accountant might help with things such as:
- annual accounts
- Self Assessment tax returns
- Corporation Tax
- tax planning
- financial reporting
- business structure
- financial advice
- dealing with more complex accounting matters

There's some overlap between the two roles, and many accountancy firms also offer bookkeeping.

But that doesn't make bookkeeping and accounting the same thing.

## An easy way to remember the difference
Imagine your business finances as a filing cabinet.

Throughout the year, invoices, payments, expenses, receipts and bank transactions keep arriving.

**Bookkeeping is about keeping that filing cabinet organised.**

Everything is recorded properly, put where it belongs and kept up to date.

**Accounting is about using the information inside it.**

Your accountant can take those organised records and use them to prepare accounts, calculate tax and help you understand the financial position of the business.

If the filing cabinet is a mess, the accountant can still work with it.

But someone has to sort it out first.

## Do I need a bookkeeper if I already have an accountant?
Possibly.

It depends on what your accountant actually does for you.

Some accountants provide bookkeeping as part of their service. Others expect you to maintain your own records and provide them with the information they need.

If you're comfortably keeping everything organised yourself, you may not need separate bookkeeping support.

But if you're regularly catching up on months of transactions before sending everything to your accountant, bookkeeping support could make that process much easier.

It's worth asking your accountant exactly what is included in their service.

## Can I do my own bookkeeping?
Absolutely.

Many sole traders and small-business owners manage their own books, particularly when the business is relatively simple.

The question isn't necessarily:

*“Am I capable of doing this?”*

A more useful question might be:

*“Is this a good use of my time?”*

If your bookkeeping takes an hour each month and you don't mind doing it, outsourcing it might not be necessary.

If receipts are piling up, transactions haven't been reconciled for months and you're spending an entire weekend getting everything ready for your accountant, that's a different situation.

Being able to do something yourself doesn't mean you always have to.

## When might a bookkeeper make sense?
Bookkeeping support might be worth considering if:
- you're regularly behind with your records
- you're not sure what's been recorded and what hasn't
- receipts and invoices are scattered across different places
- you're spending evenings catching up on transactions
- your accountant regularly has to ask for missing information
- your business has grown and there are simply more transactions to manage
- you'd rather spend that time working with customers or running the business

You don't necessarily need to wait until your bookkeeping becomes a problem.

Sometimes the best time to introduce a process is while things are still manageable.

## When might you need an accountant?
An accountant becomes particularly useful when you need help that goes beyond maintaining your records.

For example, you might want professional help with your tax return, annual accounts, tax planning or decisions that could have financial or tax consequences for your business.

That's also where it's important to recognise the limits of bookkeeping.

Keeping accurate records is valuable, but it isn't a substitute for professional accounting or tax advice when you need it.

## Could you need both?
Yes! and the two can work well together.

Your bookkeeper keeps things organised throughout the year.

Your accountant then has accurate, up-to-date information to work from when preparing accounts, dealing with tax or advising you.

Instead of handing over a year's worth of receipts, unexplained transactions and spreadsheets at the last minute, you're handing over records that have been maintained along the way.

That can make life easier for everyone involved.

## So, which one do you actually need?
Think about the problem you're trying to solve.

*“I need someone to keep my transactions and records organised.”*

You may be looking for a bookkeeper.

*“I need help with my accounts, tax or financial advice.”*

You may need an accountant.

*“I need both.”*

That's perfectly normal too.

And if you're not sure, speak to your accountant or prospective bookkeeper about exactly what they will and won't handle before deciding.
