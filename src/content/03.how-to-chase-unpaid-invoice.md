***Getting paid shouldn't feel like asking for a favour. Here's how to follow up on overdue invoices politely, professionally and without damaging your customer relationships.***

You've finished the work, sent the invoice and moved on to the next job.

Then the payment deadline comes and goes.

You check your bank account. Nothing.

Now comes the uncomfortable part: asking your customer to pay you.

It's a familiar situation for many small-business owners. You don't want to sound pushy, damage a good working relationship or make things awkward. So you give it another day. Then another week.

But here's the thing: ***following up on an unpaid invoice isn't being difficult. It's a normal part of running a business.***

The trick is to make it a routine process rather than an uncomfortable conversation.

## 1. First, check the details

Before sending a payment reminder, make sure everything is correct on your side.

Check that the invoice was sent to the right person, the payment deadline has passed and the bank details are accurate.

It's also worth checking whether the customer has already paid. Sometimes payments arrive without a clear reference, or the person responsible for approving invoices is different from your usual contact.

A quick check can save an unnecessary email.

If everything looks right, it's time to follow up.

## 2. Start with a friendly reminder

**When:** Around the payment due date or shortly afterwards.

Your first reminder doesn't need to sound like a formal demand.

People forget things. Emails get buried. Sometimes an invoice simply hasn't reached the right person.

Start by assuming there's been an oversight.

**Example email:**

> Subject: Quick reminder – Invoice [1234]
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Hope you're well.
> Just a quick reminder that invoice [1234] for £[amount] was due on [date].
> I've attached a copy for convenience.
> If you've already arranged payment, please disregard this message.
> Otherwise, could you let me know when I can expect it?
>
> Thanks!
>
> [Your name]

That's it.

No lengthy explanation. No apologies for asking. Just a polite reminder with the information they need.

## 3. Follow up if you haven't heard back

**When:** Around 7 days after the due date.

If the first reminder hasn't received a response, it's reasonable to follow up.

At this point, you can be slightly more direct while keeping the tone professional.

**Example email:**

> Subject: Follow-up – Outstanding invoice [1234]
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I'm following up on invoice [1234] for £[amount], which was due on [date].
> I haven't received payment or an update yet.
> Could you please confirm when payment will be made?
> If there's an issue with the invoice, let me know so we can get it resolved.
>
> Thanks for your help.
>
> [Your name]

Notice that you're not accusing the customer of deliberately withholding payment.

You're simply asking for an update.

That distinction matters, particularly when you're dealing with customers you hope to work with again.

## 4. Be clear when payment is significantly overdue

**When:** Around 14 days after the due date.

If you've sent reminders and still haven't received payment or a response, it's time to be firmer.

You can remain polite without leaving the situation open-ended.

**Example email:**

> Subject: Payment overdue – Invoice [1234]
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I'm writing again regarding invoice [1234] for £[amount], which was due on [date].
> The invoice remains outstanding despite my previous reminders.
> Please arrange payment by [specific date], or contact me before then if there's an issue preventing payment.
> I'd appreciate an update so we can resolve this promptly.
>
> Kind regards,
>
> [Your name]

The important difference here is that you've given a clear date for payment or a response.

You're no longer simply checking whether they've seen the invoice.

You're asking them to take action.

## 5. Know when to take the next step

Sometimes reminders aren't enough.

If a customer continues to ignore your messages, you may need to consider a more formal approach.

Depending on the circumstances, this could involve sending a formal letter before action, seeking advice about recovering the debt or considering a legal claim.

For UK business-to-business transactions, statutory late-payment interest and compensation may also apply in certain circumstances.

However, the rules depend on the transaction, your contract and the parties involved. They aren't automatically the same for business customers and consumers.

Before taking formal action, check the relevant guidance and make sure you understand your rights and obligations.

You can find information about [late commercial payments on GOV.UK.](https://www.gov.uk/late-commercial-payments-interest-debt-recovery)

*The aim isn't to escalate every late invoice. It's to have a clear process so you know what to do when a friendly reminder doesn't work.*

## 6. What if the customer says they can't pay?

Not every unpaid invoice is the result of forgetfulness.

Sometimes a customer is experiencing cash-flow difficulties. Sometimes there's a genuine dispute about the work or the amount invoiced.

If they respond and explain the situation, try to establish what's happening before deciding how to proceed.

You might need to clarify an invoice, discuss a proposed payment date or consider whether a payment arrangement is appropriate.

If you agree to different payment terms, put the agreement in writing.

Having a clear record protects both sides and helps avoid further misunderstandings.

You don't have to agree to every request, but a constructive conversation is often more productive than a series of increasingly frustrated emails.

## 7. How to make chasing invoices less awkward

One reason payment reminders feel uncomfortable is that many small-business owners handle them personally and inconsistently.

You remember an invoice hasn't been paid, feel slightly uncomfortable about mentioning it, put it off, and eventually send a message when you're already frustrated.

There's a better way.

**Make payment follow-ups part of your normal business routine.**

For example:

| When   | Action |
| :----- | -----:  |
| When sending the invoice | Include clear payment terms and a due date |
| Shortly before payment is due | Send a courtesy reminder if appropriate |
| 1–3 days overdue | Send a friendly payment reminder |
| 7 days overdue | Follow up and request a payment update |
| 14 days overdue | Send a firmer reminder with a clear response date |
| Still unpaid | Review the situation and consider appropriate next steps |


***This is an example process, not a legal timetable. Your approach should reflect your agreed payment terms, the customer relationship and the circumstances.***

The benefit is consistency.

Instead of wondering whether it's too soon to follow up, you already know when reminders should happen.

And because every customer follows the same general process, it feels less personal.

## 8. A few ways to prevent late payments in the first place

Chasing invoices is sometimes unavoidable, but there are things you can do to reduce how often it happens.

- ### 1. Make your payment terms clear
    Customers should know when payment is expected before an invoice becomes overdue.

    Agreeing on payment terms upfront avoids unnecessary confusion later.

- ### 2. Send invoices promptly
    If you wait two weeks after completing a job to send an invoice, you've already delayed the payment process.

    Try to invoice as soon as the agreed billing point is reached.

- ### 3. Make paying easy
    Include accurate payment details, the invoice number and any information the customer needs to process the payment.

    The fewer obstacles there are, the easier it is for customers to pay promptly.

- ### 4. Keep track of what's outstanding
    You shouldn't have to search through emails and bank statements every time you want to know who's paid.

    A simple invoice tracker or bookkeeping system can show which invoices are paid, unpaid or overdue.

- ### 5. Follow up consistently
    Don't wait until an invoice is several weeks overdue before checking on it.

    A regular review of outstanding invoices can help you identify late payments early.

## 9. You don't have to chase every invoice yourself
There's another option worth considering.

**Someone else can manage the administrative side of your invoicing process.**

That doesn't mean handing over control of your customer relationships or financial decisions.

It could simply mean having someone who:

- keeps your invoice records organised
- tracks payment due dates
- checks which invoices remain outstanding
- sends agreed payment reminders
- follows up on unanswered messages
- flags overdue accounts that need your attention

You remain responsible for deciding how to handle sensitive situations, disputes or further action.

But the routine work doesn't necessarily have to sit on your to-do list.

And when reminders are handled consistently, they're often less awkward for everyone involved.

## 10. Getting paid is part of doing business

Most small-business owners would rather spend their time doing the work they enjoy than checking bank transactions and writing payment reminders.

But invoicing and payment follow-ups are part of keeping a business running.

The good news is that they don't need to become a source of stress.

With clear payment terms, a consistent reminder process and organised records, you can spend less time wondering whether to chase an invoice and more time concentrating on your business.

And remember: ***you've provided the service or delivered the work. Following up on the agreed payment isn't asking for a favour.***

It's simply good business administration.
