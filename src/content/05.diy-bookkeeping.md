*Doing your own bookkeeping doesn't have to be complicated. Here's what UK sole traders need to record, what to keep and how to stay organised without turning it into another full-time job.*

You've started your own business.

You're finding customers, doing the work, sending invoices and hopefully getting paid.

Then comes the less exciting part: keeping track of the money.

Receipts, expenses, invoices, bank transactions, tax records…

It's easy to feel like you need an accounting qualification just to understand what you're supposed to be doing.

The good news? **Bookkeeping doesn't have to be complicated, especially when you're running a relatively straightforward sole trader business.**

You don't necessarily need an elaborate system or a spreadsheet with twenty different tabs.

You do, however, need accurate records.

Let's look at what that actually means.

## First, what is bookkeeping?

Bookkeeping is simply the process of recording and organising your business's financial transactions.

Money coming in.

Money going out.

And the information that explains where it came from or what it was spent on.

For a sole trader, good bookkeeping helps you understand how your business is performing, prepare information for your tax return and keep track of what you've earned and spent.

It also makes answering questions like these much easier:

- How much have I earned this month?
- How much have I spent on business expenses?
- Which customers still owe me money?
- Have I recorded all my transactions?
- How much money should I be setting aside for tax?

You don't need to become an accountant to answer these questions.

You just need a reliable way to keep track of the information.

## 1. Keep a record of your business income

Let's start with the money coming in.

As a sole trader, you need to keep records of your business sales and income.

Depending on your business, this might include payments from customers, fees for services, cash sales or income from selling products.

For each transaction, it's useful to record:

- The date
- The customer or source of income
- A description of what you sold or provided
- The amount
- The invoice or reference number, where applicable
- Whether payment has been received

For example:

| Date | Description | Amount | Status |
|------|-------------|-------:|-------:|
| 3 October | Website maintenance | £150 | Paid |
| 8 October | Garden maintenance | £80 | Paid |
| 12 October | Administrative support | £200 | Overdue |

Keeping these details together makes it easier to understand what you've earned and which payments are still outstanding.

**One important distinction:** An invoice isn't necessarily the same as money received.

For tax purposes, when you recognise income depends on the accounting method you're using. Under cash basis accounting, income is generally recorded when payment is received. Under traditional accounting, it's generally recorded when earned.

So it's useful to track both the invoice date and payment date, even if you use a simple spreadsheet.

## 2. Keep track of your business expenses

Next, the money going out.

Business expenses might include things like:

- Office supplies
- Software subscriptions
- Business insurance
- Professional fees
- Advertising and marketing
- Business travel
- Equipment
- Telephone and internet costs

Not every payment made while running a business is automatically an allowable tax expense.

Some costs have special rules, and expenses with both business and personal use may need to be apportioned.

For bookkeeping purposes, the important thing is to record what you've spent and why.

A simple expense record could look like this:

| Date | Supplier | Description | Amount |
|------|----------|------------:|-------:|
| 2 October | Software provider | Monthly subscription | £15 |
| 6 October | Stationery shop | Office supplies | £24 |
| 10 October | Insurance provider | Business insurance | £18 |

You can also add categories to make the information easier to organise.

For example, software, travel, office costs and insurance.

The aim is to avoid reaching the end of the year and wondering what half your bank transactions were for.

## 3. Keep your receipts and invoices

Recording an expense is useful.

Being able to show what it was for is just as important.

HMRC requires sole traders to keep supporting records for their business income and expenses.

These may include:

- Sales invoices
- Purchase invoices
- Receipts
- Bank statements
- Records of cash transactions
- Other documents supporting business transactions

You don't necessarily need a filing cabinet full of paper.

Digital copies can make record keeping much easier, provided they're complete, readable and retained in a way that meets the applicable requirements.

You could organise your documents into folders by tax year and month.

For example:

**Business Records → 2026–2027 → October → Receipts**

The exact folder structure doesn't matter nearly as much as being consistent.

A useful habit is to save receipts when you receive them.

Because finding a receipt from yesterday is much easier than trying to remember what you bought eight months ago.

## 4. Keep an eye on your invoices and payments

If you invoice customers, you'll want to know more than just how much you've billed.

You'll also want to know who's actually paid.

An invoice tracker can help you monitor:

- Invoice numbers
- Customer names
- Invoice dates
- Payment due dates
- Amounts
- Payments received
- Outstanding balances

For example:

| Invoice | Customer | Amount | Due date | Status |
|---------|----------|-------:|----------|--------:|
| INV-001 | Customer A | £250 | 15 October | Paid |
| INV-002 | Customer B | £180 | 20 October | Overdue |
| INV-003 | Customer C | £320 | 30 October | Awaiting payment |

This is particularly helpful when you're dealing with several customers.

Rather than searching through emails and bank statements, you can see what needs attention.

And if someone hasn't paid, you can follow up before the invoice becomes seriously overdue.

## 5. Check your records against your bank statements

This is called *bank reconciliation*.

It sounds more complicated than it is.

Essentially, you're checking that the transactions recorded in your bookkeeping system match what's actually happened in your bank account.

For example:

Your records show that a customer paid £200.

Your bank statement should show the corresponding payment.

Or you've recorded a £30 software subscription.

You should be able to identify the payment on your statement.

If something doesn't match, investigate it.

Perhaps a transaction was missed, recorded twice or entered with the wrong amount.

Reconciling regularly helps you catch these problems while they're still easy to resolve.

For a small business with relatively few transactions, a monthly check may be enough.

If you process lots of payments, you might prefer to do it weekly.

## 6. Keep personal and business money organised

As a sole trader, you and your business aren't separate legal entities in the same way that a limited company and its owner are.

That means you may be able to use a personal bank account for business transactions, subject to your bank's terms.

But mixing everything together can make bookkeeping unnecessarily difficult.

Imagine trying to identify your business expenses from a bank statement containing supermarket shopping, petrol, subscriptions, customer payments and personal transfers.

It's possible.

It's just not particularly enjoyable.

Using a separate account for business transactions can make things much easier to track.

### What about paying yourself?

As a sole trader, money you take out of the business for personal use is generally referred to as **drawings**.

It's important not to confuse drawings with business expenses.

For example, transferring £500 from your business account to your personal account doesn't automatically mean your business has incurred a £500 expense.

It's money you've withdrawn for yourself.

Keeping these transactions clearly identified helps prevent mistakes when reviewing your finances.

## 7. Know how much you're earning — and spending

Bookkeeping isn't only about preparing for tax.

It should also help you understand your business.

At the end of each month, take a few minutes to review:

**Total income:** How much money has the business earned?

**Total expenses:** How much has it cost to operate?

**Profit:** What's left after deducting the relevant business expenses?

**Outstanding invoices:** How much are customers still due to pay?

**Upcoming costs:** What payments are expected next month?

Remember that profit and the amount of money sitting in your bank account aren't necessarily the same thing.

But reviewing these figures regularly can help you make better-informed decisions.

For example, you might notice that subscriptions are costing more than expected.

Or that several customers consistently pay late.

Or that your busiest month wasn't actually your most profitable.

Those are useful things to know before the end of the tax year.

## 8. Keep records for the required period

In the UK, sole traders generally need to keep their business records for **at least five years after the 31 January Self Assessment submission deadline** for the relevant tax year.

For example, records for the 2025–2026 tax year will generally need to be kept until at least 31 January 2032.

Longer retention may be necessary in certain circumstances, such as an ongoing HMRC enquiry.

You don't normally send all your receipts and bookkeeping records to HMRC when submitting your tax return.

But you need to be able to provide supporting information if requested.

That's another reason to establish a sensible filing system early.

**Useful resource:** HMRC's guidance on [keeping records when you're self-employed](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-records?utm_source=chatgpt.com).

## 9. Understand Making Tax Digital

There's one important development UK sole traders should be aware of.

**Making Tax Digital for Income Tax (MTD)** is being introduced in stages.

Depending on your qualifying income, you may need to maintain digital records and submit quarterly updates using compatible software.

The current rollout is:

| Start date | Qualifying income |
|------------|------------------:|
| 6 April 2026 | Over £50,000 |
| 6 April 2027 | Over £30,000 |
| 6 April 2028 | Over £20,000 |

Qualifying income generally refers to gross income from self-employment and property before deducting expenses, rather than your profit.

The relevant income is assessed using an earlier tax year.

If you're within the scope of MTD, an ordinary spreadsheet on its own may not be sufficient. You'll need compatible software, which can include suitable bridging software.

**Not every sole trader is required to use MTD yet.**

Before choosing your bookkeeping system, check whether the rules apply to you.

You can find the latest requirements through [HMRC's Making Tax Digital guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/find-out-if-and-when-you-need-to-use-making-tax-digital-for-income-tax?utm_source=chatgpt.com).

## Do you need bookkeeping software?

Not necessarily.

If your business is small, your transactions are straightforward and you're not required to use Making Tax Digital, a well-maintained spreadsheet may be sufficient.

For example, you could have separate sheets for:

- Income
- Expenses
- Outstanding invoices
- Monthly summaries

As your business grows, dedicated bookkeeping software may become more useful.

It can help automate transaction imports, categorisation, reconciliation and financial reporting.

The important thing is choosing a system that meets your legal obligations and that you'll actually maintain.

**A simple system you use consistently is usually more useful than a sophisticated one you keep avoiding.**

## A simple weekly bookkeeping routine

One of the easiest ways to make bookkeeping manageable is to stop treating it as a once-a-year task.

You don't necessarily need to spend hours on it.

For a small business with relatively few transactions, a short weekly routine might be enough.

### Every week

**1. Record your income**

Check what you've invoiced and what you've received.

**2. Record your expenses**

Add any new purchases, payments or subscriptions.

**3. Save your receipts**

Make sure supporting documents are stored in the right place.

**4. Check outstanding invoices**

Identify payments that are due or overdue.

**5. Review your bank transactions**

Check that everything has been recorded correctly.

### Every month

Set aside some time to:

- Reconcile your bank account.
- Review your income and expenses.
- Check outstanding customer payments.
- Review upcoming bills.
- Correct any errors or missing transactions.
- Make sure your records are backed up.

This isn't a legal checklist for every business, but it's a practical starting point.

And once it becomes a habit, bookkeeping often feels much less intimidating.

## When might you need a bookkeeper?

Doing your own bookkeeping is perfectly reasonable.

Many sole traders manage their records themselves, particularly when they're starting out.

But as your business develops, you might find that keeping everything updated becomes increasingly time-consuming.

Perhaps you're spending evenings entering transactions.

Maybe you're regularly behind with receipts.

Or perhaps you simply don't enjoy the work and would rather spend that time serving customers.

That's when bookkeeping support might be worth considering.

A bookkeeper can help maintain your records, organise transactions, reconcile accounts and keep routine financial administration up to date.

You remain responsible for your business and its financial obligations.

But you don't necessarily need to handle every transaction yourself.

## Keep it simple. Keep it consistent.

Good bookkeeping doesn't have to involve complicated spreadsheets, expensive software or hours of paperwork.

At its heart, it's about knowing what's coming in, what's going out and keeping the records to support it.

Start with a system that suits your business.

Keep your documents organised.

Update your records regularly.

And don't wait until January to discover that you've got twelve months of receipts to sort through.

A little organisation throughout the year can save a lot of work later.
